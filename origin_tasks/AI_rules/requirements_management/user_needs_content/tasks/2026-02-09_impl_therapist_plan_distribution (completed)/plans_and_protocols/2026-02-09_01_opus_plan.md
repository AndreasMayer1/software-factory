## Execution Plan

### Agent 1: Content Writer (single agent, sequential)

This is a single-agent task. All three scenarios are independent but must be written by the same agent to ensure consistent quality and cross-referencing. The gold standard (Dr. Sarah) should be written FIRST as it sets the pattern.

#### Step 1: Create Dr. Sarah's Instruct Client on Protocol (SCEN-001-03) — GOLD STANDARD

**File**: `requirements_user_needs/personas/dr_sarah/scenarios/instruct_client_on_protocol/scenario.md`

**Narrative focus**: The didactic handover. Wednesday 2 PM session with Anna. The scenario IS the handover — not "prepare protocol then hand it over" but "the moment the paper crosses the table."

**Three-act structure**:
- **Act 1**: The session is 35 minutes in. They've been discussing Anna's vague recollections of "bad weeks." Dr. Sarah has the customized Anxiety Protocol ready (prepared yesterday — see SCEN-001-01). She decides this is the moment. The inciting incident is her reading Anna's body language and deciding she's ready.
- **Act 2**: The handover itself. Column-by-column explanation. Anna's questions ("What if I forget to fill it out?" / "What if someone at work sees this?"). Dr. Sarah's internal monologue about barrier discussion technique. The psychoeducation element — she's not just handing paper, she's teaching a skill. The 10-minute instruction where she watches Anna's comprehension vs. overwhelm in real time.
- **Act 3**: Anna folds the paper, puts it in her bag. Dr. Sarah notes what she observed during the handover — Anna's relief when blanks were normalized, her tensing at "every day." She records brief notes for next week's session. The lingering question: will Anna actually use it? Dr. Sarah has seen this moment hundreds of times. She knows the odds.

**Design implications to surface**:
- Template-based instruction generation (different protocols need different explanations)
- Barrier discussion prompts (therapist needs a structured way to anticipate obstacles)
- Privacy guidance calibrated to client's living/work situation
- Gradual expectation setting (not "every day" but "whatever you manage")
- The instruction gap: after the session, Anna has no reference for "how did she say to fill this out?"

**Quality markers**:
- Internal monologue from Dr. Sarah (professional, reflective, evidence-informed)
- Anna is present but secondary — this is Dr. Sarah's story
- Concrete time markers (how long each part takes)
- Privacy glitch: Anna glancing at the door, imagining a colleague walking in
- Reference to SCEN-001-01 (preparation) and SCEN-002-04 (Max's reception — which is actually a different client, but same handover pattern)

#### Step 2: Create Prof. Dr. Weber's Instruct Client on Protocol (SCEN-011-03)

**File**: `requirements_user_needs/personas/prof_dr_weber/scenarios/instruct_client_on_protocol/scenario.md`

**Narrative focus**: The ritual handover. Monday afternoon session with Lena. The handwritten stationery page crossing the table — the first time Prof. Dr. Weber has given a patient anything to take home in perhaps a decade.

**Three-act structure**:
- **Act 1**: Monday session, 35 minutes in. They've been exploring a dream — the corridor, the door. The feeling is gone. Prof. Dr. Weber has carried the stationery page in his notebook all weekend (prepared Friday evening — see SCEN-011-01). He has rehearsed this moment in his mind but not his words. The inciting incident is Lena saying "The feeling was gone by Thursday" — the same sentence she's said before, but today he has an answer.
- **Act 2**: He unfolds the page. Places it between them. Does NOT explain immediately — waits for her to read. His instruction is deliberately un-instructional: "This is not a form. There are no right answers." The concession about the phone — he says it looking at the stationery, not at her. Lena's question about privacy. The pivotal sentence: "You don't have to bring everything to our sessions." His internal monologue: the boundary he's crossing (reaching into between-session space), the discomfort of recommending a phone, the hope that the prompts are open enough.
- **Act 3**: Lena takes the page. She puts it in her zippered coat pocket — he notices this small gesture of care. The session ends with minimal words. He records notes after she leaves. The uncertainty: is this an invitation or an imposition? He has spent decades trusting the therapy room as the container. Today he extended the container into her week. He doesn't know if he did the right thing.

**Design implications to surface**:
- Invitation framing vs. homework framing (language matters — "practice" not "assignment")
- The therapist's handwriting as therapeutic presence (analog signal of personal investment)
- The analog-to-digital bridge problem (handwritten prompts exist on paper; what happens when the medium is digital?)
- Selective sharing ("bring what you're ready to share" — the tool must support partial disclosure)
- Non-scheduled engagement (grief doesn't follow daily tracking rhythms)

**Quality markers**:
- Prof. Dr. Weber's distinctive voice: slow, philosophical, uncomfortable with any hint of behavioral-therapeutic technique
- Lena's reactions observed through his eyes (he reads her body language differently than a behavioral therapist would)
- The weight of the gesture — he doesn't do this lightly
- The phone concession as a visible boundary-crossing moment
- Continuity with SCEN-011-01 (he prepared this page Friday evening) and SCEN-016-01 (Lena's perspective of this same session)

#### Step 3: Create Dr. med. Turan's Instruct Client on Protocol (SCEN-012-03)

**File**: `requirements_user_needs/personas/dr_med_turan/scenarios/instruct_client_on_protocol/scenario.md`

**Narrative focus**: The 90-second medical handover. Wednesday 15:00 appointment with Herr Berger. Medication switch explanation + protocol handover in under 4 minutes of appointment time.

**Three-act structure**:
- **Act 1**: 15:01, Herr Berger sits down. Dr. Turan has the customized protocol ready on the desk (prepared 12 minutes ago — see SCEN-012-01). Visual assessment: dark circles, hunched posture. The inciting incident is Berger sitting down and Dr. Turan knowing he has exactly 12 minutes before the next patient.
- **Act 2**: The handover is embedded in the medication switch conversation. First 90 seconds: why venlafaxine, what to expect, when side effects peak. Then the protocol: "Fill this out every evening. Just the numbers. Two minutes." He unfolds the paper, points to the circled side effects in the margin, underlines the safety instruction. Berger asks "Every day?" — Dr. Turan says "Ideally. Skip and continue if you miss a day." Berger asks about side effects. Dr. Turan's internal monologue: he knows Berger will fill it for 3-5 days, max. He knows the paper will end up crumpled in a jacket pocket. But the first week's data — when side effects peak — is medically critical. The "jacket pocket" moment: Berger folds the paper and puts it not in his bag but his jacket.
- **Act 3**: Berger leaves at 15:12. Dr. Turan has 3 minutes before the next patient. He does not reflect — he pulls the next file. But there is a half-second thought: "If the agitation hits in week 2 and he doesn't call..." The safety net is on the paper. Whether Berger reads it again depends on whether the paper survives his jacket pocket. Dr. Turan knows the statistics: 8 out of 10 patients return with partial data. He has built his practice around making decisions with incomplete information.

**Design implications to surface**:
- Speed-optimized handover (every second counts — the instruction must be near-zero-friction)
- Medication-specific safety instructions (different drugs = different watchlists)
- Safety escalation (if patient reports agitation/SI, someone must be notified WITHOUT waiting 4 weeks)
- Low compliance expectation (30-50% completion is the realistic baseline — design for partial data)
- Paper-KIS disconnect (the digital record doesn't know the paper exists)

**Quality markers**:
- Dr. Turan's clipped, efficient voice — no philosophy, no ritual, just clinical precision
- Time markers throughout (15:01, 15:03, 15:08, 15:12)
- The contrast with Dr. Sarah (10 min) and Prof. Weber (unlimited) is stark
- Herr Berger is a presence but not a character — Dr. Turan processes patients, not stories
- The half-second safety worry — the one human moment in an otherwise medical procedure

## Risks

1. **Narrative duplication**: The new scenarios must ADD to existing Act 3 content, not merely rewrite it. Mitigation: Focus on the therapist's internal experience and professional reasoning during the handover — territory not covered by Act 3 summaries.

2. **Gold standard too prescriptive**: If Dr. Sarah's scenario is too specific to anxiety/CBT, it won't template well for batch generation to other therapist archetypes. Mitigation: Make the structure generalizable (barrier discussion, instruction pacing, privacy check) while keeping content specific.

3. **Continuity errors**: Small details (protocol columns, session times, patient names) must match across prepare_protocol, instruct_client, and receive_protocol scenarios. Mitigation: Explicit references to source material in each step; verification checklist.

4. **Prof. Dr. Weber's handover already told from Lena's side**: SCEN-016-01 is extremely detailed about this same session moment. The new SCEN-011-03 must not contradict it but add the therapist's perspective. Mitigation: Read SCEN-016-01 as "ground truth" for observable events; SCEN-011-03 adds Prof. Weber's internal world.
