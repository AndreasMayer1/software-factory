# Persona: Dr. Sarah

## Who is Dr. Sarah?

Dr. Sarah is a licensed psychological psychotherapist (Psychologische Psychotherapeutin) specializing in behavioral therapy (Verhaltenstherapie). She runs her own practice with statutory health insurance accreditation (Kassenzulassung), seeing 20-25 patients per week in 50-minute sessions. Her patient population includes anxiety disorders, depression, and increasingly ADHD and skill deficits requiring structured interventions. 🟡

**Mental Model**: "Therapy is helping people help themselves. We need data to recognize patterns and change behavior." 🟡

**Key Quote**: "The protocol isn't for me—it's for the patient to see their own patterns. But when they bring me crumpled papers filled out in the parking lot, what patterns can we possibly find?" 🟡

## Implicit Clusters Represented

Dr. Sarah's needs and workflows also represent:
- **The Educators**: Therapists relying on homework, psychoeducation, and structured guidelines
- **The Trainers**: ADHD specialists (structure training), DBT therapists (skill chains), addiction therapists (relapse protocols)
- **The Beginners**: Therapists in training (PiA) seeking security through standardized protocols 🟡

## Jobs to Be Done

**Functional**:
- Prepare customized tracking protocols without rewriting standard elements
- Recognize patterns across multiple days/weeks of patient data
- Use session time for therapy, not administrative review
- Ensure patients complete protocols at the right time (not retroactively)

**Emotional**:
- Feel confident that session data reflects genuine patient experience
- Avoid frustration from illegible handwriting and missing data
- Trust that patient effort translates to therapeutic progress

**Social**:
- Demonstrate evidence-based practice to supervisors and insurance
- Model structured self-observation to patients and trainees

## Current Status Quo

**What Dr. Sarah does TODAY:**

She maintains Word templates on her practice PC: "Weekly Protocol," "Anxiety Diary," "Situation-Thought-Feeling Log." Before sessions or the day before, she prints these and either hands them out during the appointment or gives patients a stack of copies. 🟡

Patients return the next week with filled-out papers—often crumpled, coffee-stained, sometimes forgotten entirely. During the session, she spreads the papers on the table and manually scans them, trying to decipher handwriting and mentally connect entries: "Was the panic before or after the argument?" 🟡

For documentation, she either files the papers in the physical patient folder or laboriously scans them.

**What works:**
- ✅ No technology barrier for patients
- ✅ Tactile, physical reminder (papers on nightstand)
- ✅ No cloud or privacy concerns
- ✅ Complete control over what data is collected
- ✅ Legally compliant paper filing in locked cabinets

**What doesn't work:**
- ❌ **Parking Lot Syndrome**: Patients fill out protocols in the car 5 minutes before the session—memory distortion makes data useless 🟡
- ❌ **Pattern Blindness**: Cannot "overlay" 14 individual daily sheets to see long-term trends
- ❌ **Time Loss**: 10-15 minutes of each 50-minute session consumed by reviewing/deciphering entries
- ❌ **Compliance Failures**: Patients forget protocols at home → no data foundation for the session
- ❌ **Handwriting Issues**: Tired or rushed patients produce illegible entries
- ❌ **Redundant Work**: Writes identical instructions ("Scale 0-10, note situation") by hand for every client

## Trigger & Context

| Trigger | State | Environment |
|---------|-------|-------------|
| Preparing protocols | Focused, planning | Office desk, 5-10 min before first patient |
| Handing out protocols | Instructive, hopeful | Therapy room, end of session |
| Receiving protocols | Hopeful, then often frustrated | Therapy room, start of session |
| Deciphering entries | Analytical, time-pressured | Therapy room, papers on table, patient watching |

## Barriers

**Fears**:
- Digital alternatives that sync to cloud without explicit control
- Technology creating distance from patients (screen vs. eye contact)
- GDPR violations from accidentally exposing patient data
- Learning curve for new systems with no time for training
- Becoming dependent on technology that might fail

**Inertia**:
- Current paper solution works and is legally unassailable
- Years of accumulated Word templates represent significant investment
- Some patients (older, tech-averse) explicitly prefer paper
- "Good enough"—therapy works despite the inefficiencies

## Anti-Traits

This persona is NOT:
- A psychiatrist prescribing medication (she does psychotherapy only)
- Someone who analyzes data alone (reviews WITH patient in session)
- A tech enthusiast wanting features for features' sake
- Interested in gamification or consumer app aesthetics
- Looking for AI to replace her clinical judgment
- Wanting extensive analytics dashboards she'd review alone
- Comfortable with patient data leaving her physical control

# Persona: Prof. Dr. Weber

## Who is Prof. Dr. Weber?

Prof. Dr. Weber is a depth psychologist and psychoanalyst with decades of experience. He maintains a private practice (Privatpraxis) or an older statutory insurance seat (Kassensitz), working with patients on long-term explorations of the unconscious, relational patterns, and meaning-making. He sees 12-15 patients weekly in 50-minute sessions, often over years of treatment. 🟡

**Mental Model**: "The computer has no place in the therapy room. What matters is the unspoken, dreams, and free associations—not checkboxes and numbers." 🟡

**Key Quote**: "When a patient tells me their dream from Tuesday, I don't want data points. I want to feel what they felt. A form cannot capture the uncanny." 🟡

## Implicit Clusters Represented

Prof. Weber's stance and needs also represent:
- **The Tech-Skeptics**: All therapists who experience technology as a disturbance to the therapeutic relationship
- **The Narratives**: Systemic therapists, gestalt therapists focused on text, imagery, and story rather than numbers
- **The Somatics**: Body-oriented therapists who might use "body maps" (drawing sensations on body outlines)
- **The Data Protectors**: Practitioners with extreme requirements for local-only data storage 🟡

## Jobs to Be Done

**Functional**:
- Access patients' between-session experiences in their authentic emotional form
- Preserve the "felt sense" of dreams and memories, not just factual content
- Bridge the gap between sessions without disrupting the transference relationship
- Document therapeutic process for supervision without digital exposure

**Emotional**:
- Maintain the sanctity of the therapeutic space (analog, human, present)
- Feel included in younger patients' inner lives (not excluded by their devices)
- Trust that sensitive material remains absolutely private

**Social**:
- Be seen as a thoughtful practitioner, not a Luddite
- Maintain professional credibility while holding technological boundaries

## Current Status Quo

**What Prof. Weber does TODAY:**

He works exclusively with fountain pen and leather-bound notebook. His therapy room has no computer, no tablet, no visible technology. He asks patients to "keep a diary" (a free-form notebook) or to write down dreams upon waking. 🟡

During sessions, patients sometimes read aloud from their notebooks. He listens, makes notes in his own book, and together they explore associations and meanings. His documentation is handwritten, stored in locked filing cabinets, never digitized. 🟡

**What works:**
- ✅ No technology interferes with presence and attunement
- ✅ Handwriting slows thought, encouraging reflection
- ✅ Complete privacy—no servers, no breaches, no "cloud"
- ✅ The ritual of pen and paper carries symbolic weight
- ✅ Patients feel the therapeutic space is protected and sacred

**What doesn't work:**
- ❌ **Media Break with Gen Z**: Younger patients (20s-30s) use smartphone note apps, voice memos, even TikTok drafts to process experiences—he cannot access this "black box" and feels excluded from their actual processing 🟡
- ❌ **Emotional Memory Decay**: "The vibe is gone"—patients can recall facts about a dream from 5 days ago but have lost the emotional texture that matters therapeutically 🟡
- ❌ **Cloud Anxiety**: He would consider digital tools but trusts no provider ("Everything ends up on American servers") and lacks technical knowledge to evaluate claims 🟡
- ❌ **No Transitional Object**: He cannot give patients anything to hold onto between sessions during crises—no way to extend therapeutic presence into the week 🟡
- ❌ **Illegibility Over Time**: His own handwritten notes from years ago are sometimes difficult to decipher

## Trigger & Context

| Trigger | State | Environment |
|---------|-------|-------------|
| Patient mentions "I wrote something in my Notes app" | Curious but excluded, slightly helpless | Therapy room, patient holding phone |
| Patient struggles to recall dream emotions | Frustrated, sensing lost opportunity | Therapy room, mid-session |
| Reading about data breaches in news | Vindicated but also anxious | Home, newspaper |
| Patient in crisis between sessions | Concerned, limited options | Phone call, evening hours |

## Barriers

**Fears**:
- Technology destroying the quality of therapeutic presence
- Patient data being harvested, sold, or exposed through breaches
- Becoming dependent on systems he doesn't understand
- Appearing old-fashioned or out of touch to younger colleagues
- Digital tools reducing rich experience to simplistic metrics

**Inertia**:
- Decades of successful practice without technology
- Genuine philosophical conviction about analog's value
- No trusted guide to navigate the technology landscape
- Patients who sought him out specifically value his approach

## Anti-Traits

This persona is NOT:
- A behavioral therapist interested in protocols or structured homework
- Someone who wants graphs, trends, or quantified outcomes
- Comfortable with any cloud-based storage
- Looking to "optimize" therapeutic efficiency
- Interested in shortcuts to insight (values the slow process)
- Wanting to monitor patients between sessions (respects autonomy)
- A technophobe in personal life (uses smartphone, email—just not in therapy)

# Persona: Dr. med. Turan

## Who is Dr. med. Turan?

Dr. med. Turan is a psychiatrist (Facharzt für Psychiatrie) working in a Medical Care Center (MVZ) or clinic outpatient department. He manages a high patient volume: 40+ patients per week in 10-20 minute appointments. His focus is medication management, crisis intervention, and bridging gaps until psychotherapy slots open. He makes consequential medical decisions under severe time pressure. 🟡

**Mental Model**: "I need facts for medical decisions. Is the medication working? Is the patient sleeping? Are they suicidal? I have three minutes to decide." 🟡

**Key Quote**: "When I ask 'How was your week?' and they say 'Okay, I guess,' that tells me nothing. But I can't spend 10 minutes excavating—the next patient is already waiting." 🟡

## Implicit Clusters Represented

Dr. Turan's needs and constraints also represent:
- **The Medics**: Ward physicians, consultation services needing quick overviews
- **The Risk Managers**: Forensic psychiatrists, crisis services tracking suicidality and aggression
- **The Data Collectors**: Researchers, burnout coaches wanting interfaces to wearables and objective data
- **The Efficiency Optimizers**: All practitioners who have no time for narrative prose 🟡

## Jobs to Be Done

**Functional**:
- Assess medication efficacy quickly and objectively
- Identify deterioration or crisis between appointments
- Make safe prescribing decisions with minimal guesswork
- Document encounters efficiently for legal and insurance requirements

**Emotional**:
- Feel confident that decisions are medically sound, not just hunches
- Reduce anxiety about missing suicidality or other risks
- Avoid burnout from information-extraction labor in every appointment

**Social**:
- Demonstrate evidence-based prescribing to colleagues and insurance
- Build enough trust with patients for honest reporting despite brief contacts
- Maintain professional standing despite assembly-line conditions

## Current Status Quo

**What Dr. Turan does TODAY:**

He uses an outdated clinic information system (KIS) on a desktop computer. The interface is clunky, optimized for billing rather than clinical insight. Each appointment follows a verbal protocol: "How is sleep? Mood from 1-10? Energy? Appetite? Any thoughts of harming yourself?" 🟡

Patients answer vaguely: "Sleep? I don't know, okay I guess." "Mood? Maybe a 5?" He types fragments into the KIS, makes medication decisions partly on intuition, and moves to the next patient. Between quarterly follow-ups, he has no visibility into what actually happened. 🟡

**What works:**
- ✅ Structured questioning provides some baseline data
- ✅ KIS satisfies billing and legal documentation requirements
- ✅ High throughput keeps wait times manageable for patients
- ✅ Experience-based intuition often correct despite data poverty

**What doesn't work:**
- ❌ **Blind Flying**: No visibility between appointments—misses medication non-adherence, manic phases, gradual deterioration 🟡
- ❌ **Inefficient Data Gathering**: Spends precious minutes asking basic status questions every visit instead of reviewing trends 🟡
- ❌ **Subjective vs. Objective Gap**: Patient says "feeling better" but objective markers might tell different story (sleep data, activity levels) 🟡
- ❌ **Safety Blind Spots**: Often learns about suicidal ideation only when crisis already escalating—no early warning system 🟡
- ❌ **Recall Distortion**: Patients genuinely can't remember two weeks accurately ("last Tuesday? which Tuesday?")
- ❌ **Vague Answers**: "Okay I guess" provides no actionable signal

## Trigger & Context

| Trigger | State | Environment |
|---------|-------|-------------|
| Starting new medication | Needs baseline, concerned about side effects | Office, desktop, patient seated |
| Quarterly follow-up | Time-pressured, many patients waiting | Office, end of long day |
| Patient in crisis call | Alarm, limited information | Phone, between appointments |
| Post-hospitalization check | Vigilant, transition is risky | Office, patient freshly discharged |

## Barriers

**Fears**:
- Missing a suicidal patient and facing consequences (professional, personal, legal)
- Making medication errors due to incomplete information
- Patients feeling like "just a number" in high-volume practice
- Additional tools creating more work rather than less
- Data systems that don't integrate with existing KIS

**Inertia**:
- Current system is "known evil"—flaws are familiar
- No time to learn new tools during packed clinic days
- Institutional barriers to changing clinic workflows
- Patients conditioned to brief, question-answer format

## Anti-Traits

- Someone with time for extensive patient education
- Looking for features that extend appointments
- Interested in patient "engagement" or motivation design
- Comfortable with data outside secure clinical systems
- Wanting to replace medical judgment with algorithms
- Able to dedicate time to patient onboarding/training
